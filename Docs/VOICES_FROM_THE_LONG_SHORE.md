# Voices from the Long Shore

A collection of accounts from the dead of Jörmund. Carved in stone, scratched into bark, spoken to the dirt.

---

## I. Almost

I was ten steps from the water when it stopped.

I had been walking for three days. Not because the lake was far. It was not far. I could see it from the ridge above Wickbarrow. But the walk to the Pool is not a walk you rush. You go slowly. You feel the pull in your chest, gentle, warm, pointing you forward, and you let it set the pace. It does not hurry you. It does not drag. It asks. And you follow, the way you follow a song you can almost hear, leaning into it, trying to catch the words.

I had waited two hundred years. I had been a fisherman in life, and on the shore I was nothing, and I was tired of being nothing, and when the pull came it was the first time in two hundred years that I knew where I was going. I packed no bag. There is nothing to bring. I said my name one last time to the dirt outside my shelter and I walked.

The water was still when I reached the lake. Not still the way a pond is still. Still the way a held breath is still. The surface was flat and grey and it reflected the sky, and the sky reflected the surface, and between the two reflections there was a depth that I could feel in my teeth. The pull was strongest here, right here, at the edge, and it said: step in. Just step in. You are ready. The thread is ready. Somewhere a child is about to be born and the child needs what you are carrying, and all you have to do is walk forward and let the water take the weight.

I lifted my foot.

The pull vanished.

It did not fade. It did not recede the way a tide recedes. It was there and then it was not, the way a voice stops when the speaker falls dead. One moment the warm pressure in my chest was pointing me forward and the next moment there was nothing, just my own heartbeat, which is not a heartbeat because I am dead and my heart does not beat, it is just the habit of a chest expanding and contracting because it does not know how to stop.

I stood there with my foot raised over the water for a long time. I put it down. I waited. I waited an hour. A day. A week. I sat at the edge of the lake and I waited for the pull to come back, and it did not come back, and it has not come back, and that was three hundred years ago.

I have not moved from this shore. I eat the dim food that the others bring me and I sleep against the same rock and I watch the water and it does not stir. Sometimes I think I feel something, a faint warmth, a whisper of the old pull, and I lean forward and hold my breath and it is nothing. It is the memory of the pull, which is not the same thing, the way the memory of a meal does not fill the stomach.

I was so close. Ten steps. Maybe fewer. I could see my reflection in the water, and the reflection was already starting to blur, which is what happens when the Pool begins to accept you. The edges of my face were softening. I was beginning to be unmade. And then the pull stopped and my reflection sharpened and I was solid again, whole and heavy and alone, and the water was just water, and the sky was just the sky, and I have been sitting here ever since.

I do not know what happened. The others say a man refused. A tall man, a king, someone who said no and broke something that cannot be fixed. I do not know if this is true. I know only that I was ten steps from the end of everything and now I am here and the water is still and my name is Ketil and I say it to the dirt every morning and every morning it means a little less.

## II. The Tall Man at the Arch

He came through in winter. I remember because the frost was thick on the arch that day and the stone cracked when he touched it, the way hot iron cracks ice. He was tall. Broader than most. He walked through the arch the way a man walks through the door of his own hall, not confused, not stumbling, not reaching out to touch the stone the way the new ones always do. He looked at the sky and he looked at the ground and he looked south and then he looked north and he nodded once, to himself, the way a builder nods when he sees the foundation of the house he is about to raise.

I thought nothing of it. I have stood at this arch for a long time and I have seen ten thousand souls walk through, and each one is a story I will never read, and I have learned not to read them. The confused ones weep. The angry ones shout. The old ones sit down and close their eyes. The children run. This man did none of those things. He walked. He walked north, toward the center, at the pace of a man who knows exactly where he is going.

I should have spoken to him. I should have said what I say to every new arrival: the water is ahead, the path is long, the towns will feed you. I did not say it. Something in the way he walked made me feel that he did not need to be told where the water was. He already knew. He had already decided what he would do when he got there, and the decision was older than his arrival, older than his death, baked into the set of his shoulders and the length of his stride. He was not walking toward the Pool. He was walking toward the island. There is a difference, and I should have seen it.

It was a hundred years before I understood what I had watched walk past me. A hundred years before the pull stopped and the water went still and the dead began to pile up on the shore like driftwood after a storm. By then the tall man was a king with a tower and an army and a name that no one would say aloud because saying it gave it weight and weight was the last thing any of us needed.

I could have spoken to him. I did not.

I have been wondering for three hundred years whether it would have mattered.

## III. For My Mother

I am writing this on the stone beside the South Arch because I do not know how to say it to her face.

Mother. I am going into the water. I am going in alone. I am sorry.

You have been standing at the North Arch for forty years, waiting for us, and every time one of us came through you held us and you wept and you said we would go together. I believed you. Dalla believed you. Einar believed you. We sat in the camp you built and we waited and we told each other that the family would walk into the Pool as one, and the water would take us as one, and wherever we went next we would carry a trace of each other.

I believed it until the pull came.

I cannot explain what it feels like to someone who has refused it. It is not a voice. It is not a command. It is a warmth in the chest that says: there is something ahead. Not behind. Not here. Ahead. And the something ahead is not frightening. It is not an ending. It is a door that is open and warm and the room beyond it is full of light, and all you have to do is walk through, and the walking is easy, and the light is kind.

You told me to refuse. You told me the pull would pass. You told me the family comes first. You were wrong, Mother. Not wrong about the family. Wrong about the pull. It does not pass. It sits in the chest and it waits and every morning it is a little warmer and every night the room beyond the door is a little brighter and after six months of feeling it I cannot sleep because the light is all I see when I close my eyes.

I am not choosing the water over you. I am choosing the door because the door is open and I am afraid that if I wait it will close and I will be here forever, in this grey place, under this grey sky, with nothing ahead and nothing behind and the only warmth I have ever felt on this island fading because I was too afraid to follow it.

I love you. I love Dalla and Einar and the little one whose name I am already forgetting because the pull does that, it makes everything behind you blur, the way a landscape blurs when you are running toward something bright.

I left this at the South Arch because you will not come south. You will not leave the North Arch because you are waiting for the rest of us. I do not think the rest of us are coming, Mother. I think Dalla is feeling the pull too. I saw her face yesterday and she had the look, the one where the eyes go soft and the mouth goes quiet and the hands stop fidgeting because the hands know they are about to let go of everything.

Do not hate us. We are not leaving you. We are walking forward, the way the water asks, and we cannot carry you with us because you will not be carried.

Say my name tomorrow morning and I will hear it from wherever I am.

I am your son. My name is Arne. I am going in.

## IV. A Year in the Crucible

The Sovereign walked past the shelters again last night. Third time this week. She does not see us. I do not think she sees anything that is not fire.

She is taller than the shelters. When she passes, the heat dries the moss off the stones and the black blood in my arms goes thin and fast and my skin tightens the way hide tightens over a drum. She smells like the inside of a kiln. The dead who live in the outer ring say she hums, a low sound, not a song exactly but the sound a forge makes when the coals are right and the bellows are still. I have never been close enough to hear it. I do not want to be close enough to hear it.

She has been here longer than anything. The old woman down the hill says the Sovereign was sitting at Brann before the first camp was built, before the arches were smooth, before the dead had a name for the grey food they eat every morning. I do not know if this is true. The old woman says many things.

Living here is simple. You learn the routes. She walks the north ridge at dusk, if dusk is the right word for the hour when the light dims slightly and the glow from the crack gets brighter. She circles the lava lake twice and then walks east along the rim and then she is gone for a day, sometimes two, somewhere deep in the mountain where the rock is liquid and the air ripples. You build in the gaps. You eat in the gaps. You sleep, if you can, in the gaps. The heat makes sleep strange. I dream of cool things. Rivers. Snow. A woman's hand on my forehead. I do not remember whose hand.

We had a new arrival last month. A young man from the West Arch, burnt in life, drawn to the warmth the way they always are. He asked why we live here. Why we do not move south to Storsprunga where the air is cool and the dead walk upright and there are walls and baths and a fortune-teller who reads the runes for a handful of soul-fragments.

I told him: the heat is honest. It does not promise anything. It does not whisper of water or pulling or doors that open into light. It is simply hot, and the hotness is real, and on an island where nothing else is real the hotness is enough.

He looked at me the way the new ones always look at the old ones. He will leave in a month. They always leave. And then one day, years from now, they will come back, because the coast is pretty and the towns are gentle and the fortune-tellers are kind, but none of it is warm, not truly warm, not in the way that the Crucible is warm, which is the warmth of a thing that could kill you and chooses not to, and that restraint is the closest thing to mercy I have found on this shore.

## V. My Name

My name is Sigrid.

I write it here because I woke this morning and it took me a moment to remember it. Not long. A breath. Maybe two. But it has never taken me a breath before. It has always been there, the first thing, the thing I say to the dirt before my eyes are open, the word that keeps me upright and speaking and shaped like a woman instead of shaped like a thing that crawls.

My name is Sigrid. I was born in a valley. I had a husband. His name was. His name. He had dark hair. I think. I think he had dark hair and I think he laughed in a way that made the dogs bark and I think there were dogs and I think the valley was green but I am not certain about the green. The green may be something I borrowed from someone else's story. We share stories here and after a while the stories bleed into each other and I cannot always tell which memories are mine and which I took from a dead woman who sat next to me at a fire a hundred years ago and told me about her valley which may have been green or may have been brown or may not have existed at all.

My name is Sigrid. I say it every morning. I carve it into the stone beside my bed every night. The carvings are deep. I can feel them with my fingers in the dark. S-I-G-R-I-D. Six cuts. Six sounds. One woman.

This morning it took me a breath.

Tomorrow it may take me two.

I have seen what happens when the name goes. I have watched it happen to others. The eyes go first. Not blind. White. The white of a thing that no longer needs to see because seeing is for people who have somewhere to look and the nameless have nowhere to look and so the eyes close from the inside. Then the back bends. Then the jaw. Then the hands, which were made for holding and carrying and touching, curl inward and become something else, something that grips and tears and does not remember what it was made for.

I do not want to become that. I do not want to crawl.

My name is Sigrid. My name is Sigrid. My name is Sigrid.

If I carve it deep enough, will the stone remember me when I cannot remember myself?

## VI. Stone Upon Stone

I have been laying stone for the king for sixty years. I was a mason in life. I built walls and hearths and the stone foundation of a church that I did not believe in but whose lines I admired. My hands know stone the way a mother's hands know a child's face: by touch, by weight, by the grain of it, by the places where it wants to split and the places where it wants to hold.

The king's tower is the finest thing I have ever built. I will say that plainly because it is plainly true. In life I built for men who wanted things quickly and cheaply and did not care whether the corners were true. On this shore I build for a man who has nothing but time and wants the corners perfect, and the freedom of that, the luxury of doing a thing right because there is no reason to do it wrong, is the closest thing to joy I have found since I died.

The walls are fitted dry. No mortar. No fill. Stone against stone, cut so precisely that water cannot find the seam. I know this because I poured water on the joints and it ran off the way it runs off a living thing's skin, finding no crack, no gap, no way in. The dead who helped me cut the stone, forty of us at the peak, worked without rest because the dead do not tire, and the work was the rest, and the rest was the work, and I do not know a better way to say it than that.

The throne took me eleven years. A single block. The king wanted it carved from one piece, no joins, no patches, and the block we found in the eastern quarry was almost right but not quite, and so I spent three years finding the right block and eight years carving it, and when it was finished the king sat in it and said nothing, and the nothing was enough.

I am proud of the tower. I am afraid of the man who sits in it.

I have watched him change. Sixty years ago, when I laid the first course of the outer wall, he came down to the foundation and put his hand on the stone and I saw his hand and it was a man's hand, broad and rough and brown. Last month he came down to inspect the new stairwell and he wore gloves, and beneath the gloves I saw the shape of something that was not a hand, not quite, the fingers too long and too jointed and the nails too dark, and he caught me looking and he pulled the glove tight and he walked away and neither of us said anything.

I am a mason. I build what I am told to build. But I am building a tower for something that is not a man anymore, and the tower is perfect, and the thing inside it is not, and I do not know what happens when the tower outlasts the king.

## VII. What the Metal Wants

You do not forge on this island the way you forge in the living world. I learned that the hard way, heating a bar of the dark stone to yellow and swinging the hammer and watching the bar crumble to powder under the blow. The stone is not iron. It is not bronze. It is not any metal I knew in life. It looks like iron. It sounds like iron when you strike it, a clean high ring that carries across the workshop. But it does not behave like iron, because it is not made of the things iron is made of. It is made of the island, which is made of the void, and the void does not care about hammers.

You have to ask it.

I spent sixty years learning how to ask. The trick is heat, but not the heat of the forge. The forge heat gets the bar soft enough to shape, yes, but the shaping only holds if you are thinking the right thing while you swing. I do not mean that in a mystical way. I mean it literally. If you heat a bar and think of nothing and swing, the bar crumbles. If you heat a bar and think of the sword you are trying to make, hold the image of it in your head, the curve of the blade, the weight of the tang, the way it should sit in the hand, and then you swing, the bar moves. It listens. It shapes itself toward the image in your mind, the way clay shapes itself toward the potter's hands, and the closer the image is to something real, something you have held and used and understood, the better the shaping holds.

This is why I make good weapons and Keld in Nasus makes decent ones and the amateurs make garbage. It is not skill, exactly. It is memory. I have held more tools in my life than Keld has, and I remember them more clearly, and when I pour a shape into the bar the bar has more to work with. A smith who was a farmer in life and never held a sword cannot forge a good sword on this island, because the image in his head is a guess, and guesses crumble.

The other thing the metal responds to is the residue. The soul-fragments. You grind them to powder and mix them into the quench, and the bar drinks them up the way parched soil drinks rain, and whatever charge the fragments carry settles into the grain of the metal and stays there. A weapon quenched with fragments from a soul that died in fire will carry heat. A weapon quenched with fragments from a soul that froze will carry frost. I do not know why this works. The fragments remember, I think. They carry the last feeling of the soul they came from, and the metal, which is hungry for memory because it has none of its own, takes what it is given and holds it.

I have been forging for nine hundred years and I still do not fully understand what the metal is. But I know what it wants. It wants to be something. Anything. The void is formless and the metal is born from the void and it hates the formlessness the way a child hates the dark. Give it a shape and a memory and a name, and it will hold. Take those things away and it is dust again.

The dead are the same way, if you think about it long enough. But I try not to think about it.

## VIII. Warm

I have washed ten thousand dead. Their skin is cool. Their blood is black and thick and smells of ash and old leaves. I know the feel of a dead body the way a baker knows the feel of dough: the give of it, the temperature, the way it resists the water and then surrenders.

The stranger walked into my bath-house on a day like any other. I had a basin of hot water and a cloth and the same tired routine I have performed for twelve hundred years. I reached for the stranger's wrist to guide them to the bench and I stopped.

The wrist was warm.

Not warm the way the water is warm, heated by coals and effort. Warm the way bread is warm when it comes out of the oven. Warm the way a child is warm when you hold it against your chest and feel the small heart going. Warm from the inside. Warm because something inside the stranger was alive, actually alive, pumping red blood through veins that worked, and the warmth of that blood was radiating through the skin and into my hand and I stood there holding the stranger's wrist and I could not let go because I had forgotten what warmth felt like and the remembering was so sudden and so total that it knocked the breath out of me, which is not a real breath because I am dead but the body remembers breathing the way it remembers everything else, by reflex, by habit, by the ghost of a life it can no longer live.

The stranger looked at me. I looked at the stranger. I let go of the wrist.

I did not say what I was thinking. I did not say: you are alive. I did not say: your blood is red and your skin is warm and you do not belong here and whatever brought you to this shore has made a terrible mistake or a terrible choice and I do not know which is worse. I did not say any of that. I said: the water is ready. Sit.

The stranger sat. I washed them. The water ran red where it touched a cut on the stranger's arm, red and thin and bright, not the thick black that runs off the dead like old oil. I watched the red swirl in the basin and I thought: I have not seen that color in twelve hundred years.

Later, after the stranger left, I poured the water out behind the bath-house. The dirt where the red water soaked in is darker than the dirt around it. Not black. Dark red, like wine stains on linen. I go out and look at it sometimes, when the grey of this place gets too heavy. It is the only color on the island that did not come from fire or frost or the faint glow of the crack behind Storsprunga.

Red. Such a small word for such a large thing to feel.

## IX. The Boy at the Forge

I watch the boy from the ridge above Brann, the way you watch a fire from the far side of the river: close enough to feel the heat, too far to burn.

He has been hammering for three hundred years. I know this because I have been watching for two hundred of them, and the old woman at the Crucible says he was hammering for a century before I arrived. He does not stop. He does not eat. He does not sleep, or if he does, he sleeps standing, with the hammer in his hand and the blade on the anvil and the fire at his back.

The blade is always wrong. I can see it from here, the way the metal twists when he pulls it from the quench, the way the edge warps and the tang bends and the whole thing falls apart in his hands like wet clay. He stares at it. He puts it back in the fire. He starts again. Every time, every single time, there is a moment just after the quench when his face changes, when the hope rises, and then the blade warps and the hope falls and his face goes flat and he puts it back in the fire.

I went down once. I stood at the edge of the forge and I watched him work and I said: the metal will not hold. It is not iron. It does not remember the way iron remembers. You are pouring your image into a thing that cannot keep it. He did not look at me. He said: it held yesterday. It held for a breath before it cracked. Tomorrow it will hold for two breaths. I said: and then? He said: and then I will make it hold for three.

I left. There is nothing to say to a man who measures hope in breaths.

His master must have been something. A boy does not spend three hundred years hammering a blade that will not hold unless the master who set the task was worth the work. I do not know the master's name. The boy calls himself Sindri's son, but the way he says it, the way the name sits in his mouth, it is not a name. It is a hand on his shoulder. It is a voice saying: again. Try again. You are close.

He is not close. He will never be close. The metal is not iron and the island is not a forge and the blade he is trying to make does not exist in any world that this place can build. But the trying is all he has, and the trying is the thing that keeps him upright and speaking and shaped like a boy instead of shaped like the things that crawl in the woods, and so I do not go down again and I do not tell him the truth and I watch from the ridge and I hope, in the way that the dead hope, which is quietly, and without expectation, that tomorrow the blade holds for two breaths instead of one.

## X. What the Arch Remembers

I am not the oldest soul on this shore but I am close. I have stood at this arch for longer than the towns have had names. I have watched ten thousand souls walk through and I have said the same words to each of them and I have watched them walk north and I have not followed.

The arch is warm. Not hot. Not like the Crucible or the crack behind Storsprunga. Warm the way a hand is warm when someone you love puts it on your face. It is the warmth of passage, of all the souls who have touched this stone on their way through, and the stone remembers them all, and the remembering generates a heat that never fades, and I stand in that heat the way a cold man stands by a fire, because the alternative is the shore, and the shore is grey and still and cold.

I have tried to leave. I have walked away a dozen times. I have gone as far as Wickbarrow, two days' walk, and sat among the dead and listened to their conversations and tried to care about the things they care about, the food and the baths and the wall and the fortune-teller and the man in the tower. I cannot care. I walk back to the arch and I put my hand on the stone and the warmth comes back and I stand there and I watch and I wait.

I am waiting for something I cannot name. I think I am waiting for the arch to open the other way. I think I am waiting for a door that goes out instead of in, a passage back to the world I came from, a chance to feel the sun that this sky only pretends to hold. I know this will not happen. The arches go one way. They have always gone one way. But I stand here and I wait, because the waiting is the only shape my self has left, and if I stop waiting I will have no shape at all, and the things with no shape crawl in the woods and howl at the sky, and I am not ready to howl.

Not yet.

I saw a stranger come through the arch recently. Not a dead stranger. A living one. The stranger's blood was red and the stranger's skin was warm and the arch cracked and groaned when the stranger passed through it, the way a door groans when it is opened from the wrong side.

I said what I always say. The water is ahead. The path is long.

I did not say the rest. I did not say: you are the first living thing to walk through this arch in ten thousand years and the stone screamed when you touched it and I do not know if the scream was joy or pain and I do not know why you are here but whatever sent you sent you for a reason and the reason is heavier than either of us can carry alone.

I said: the towns will feed you.

And the stranger walked north.
