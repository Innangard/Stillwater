# Jörmund: Expanded Story Bible

A deep-lore companion to the working Story Bible. Everything in the original bible is canonical. This document grows outward from those roots, reaching back to the oldest events and forward to the present moment, organized from the widest lens to the tightest.

---

## Part I: The Shape of Things Before

### 1. Ginnungagap and the First Silence

Before the worlds were named, there was Ginnungagap. The yawning void. The space between the fires of Muspelheim and the frost of Niflheim. It was not nothing. It was the silence before the first word, the held breath before the scream. Norse cosmology teaches that the meeting of fire and frost inside the void created Ymir, and from Ymir's body the gods shaped the earth. That story is true, so far as the living world is concerned.

But the dead of Jörmund know something the living do not. Ginnungagap was not emptied when the worlds were made. It was merely pressed to the edges. It still exists, a membrane of un-being that separates every world from every other world, thin as breath, cold as the instant before thought. Jörmund was built inside a fold of Ginnungagap, not in the void itself, but in a pocket where the void had been pushed aside to make room for a threshold. The Norns chose this fold because nothing else could survive there, which meant nothing could interfere.

This is why the sky over Jörmund does not move. It is not a sky in the way the living world has a sky. It is the inner surface of a fold in the void, a pale membrane that reflects light without producing it. The sun does not cross Jörmund because no sun was ever placed there. What the dead see when they look up is the afterimage of the light they carried with them when they died, a collective hallucination sustained by the memory of ten thousand skies. Most people die during the day. The aggregate of their remembered light settles on an average somewhere between noon and dusk, and so Jörmund exists in an eternal grey afternoon that never tilts toward evening.

The sky changing color in the final act is not a weather event. It is the memory of the dead shifting. When the Pool stirs and hope returns to the island, the dead begin to remember evenings and mornings and sunsets, and the sky responds. The first color shift is gold. The second is rose. By the time the player reaches the King's Tower, the sky is the deep blue of a winter evening, the first time the dead have collectively remembered the dark.

### 2. The Norns and the Weaving of Jörmund

The Norns, Urðr, Verðandi, and Skuld, are not gods. They predate the gods. They predate the worlds. In the old stories, they tend Yggdrasil's roots and water them from the Well of Urðr, carving the fates of all things into the bark. Those stories are true, but they are told from the perspective of the living, who see only the part of the Norns' work that touches their world. The greater portion of their labor has always been invisible: the weaving of the passage between.

Death, in the cosmology of Jörmund, is not an ending. It is a transition point, a place where the thread of a soul must be cut from one loom and wound onto another. Urðr holds the thread that was, the complete life from birth to final breath. Verðandi holds the thread that is, the present moment of the soul's existence in the passage. Skuld holds the thread that shall be, the next life, the new vessel, the infant who will open its eyes and carry the weight of a soul it does not remember.

The three of them built Jörmund together, in an age before the gods took their thrones.

Urðr laid the foundation. She carved the island from a spur of Ginnungagap's frozen edge, shaping stone and soil from the residue of unmade things. The dirt of Jörmund is not earth. It is compressed void, dark and fine, which is why it is always slightly too dark, slightly too cold to the touch. She shaped the coastline so that it would face inward, toward the center, because the purpose of the shore was always to guide souls toward the Pool. Every path on Jörmund, if walked long enough, leads to the center. This is not coincidence. It is architecture.

Verðandi filled it with the present. She gave the island its quality of now, the ability to sustain a soul in its current form without degrading it. She is the reason the dead can eat and sleep and speak and feel on the long shore. Without her work, a soul separated from its body would dissolve instantly into the void. Verðandi's weaving holds the dead together, gives them substance, gives them the black blood that is not blood but memory of blood, dark and slow. She also planted the grasses, the pale trees, the dim food that sustains without nourishing. These are not living things. They are the idea of living things, woven from Verðandi's sense of what a soul would need to feel at home in a place that is not home.

Skuld shaped the Pool. What the Pool is made of, no one can say. It looks like water and feels like water when you stand at its edge, but the dead who have studied it for centuries say it is something older than water, something that was here before the Norns arrived or perhaps something the Norns found in the deep folds of the void and gave purpose to. When a soul steps into the Pool, it is unmade, gently and completely, and the raw thread is wound onto the next loom, the loom of what shall be. Somewhere in the living world, an infant draws its first breath, and the cycle turns.

The Pool does not force. It does not pull. It does not drag. A soul must walk into it freely, must feel the quiet tug behind its eyes and choose to follow it, must step forward of its own will and let the water take the weight. Anything else would be violence, and the threshold was built to be a mercy, not a prison.

This is the tragedy at the root of everything. The gentleness that made Jörmund kind is the same gentleness that let Hjarrandi break it.

### 3. The Arches

The four arches at the cardinal points of the island are part of the Norns' original design, doorways woven into the membrane of Ginnungagap at the same time the shore and the Pool were made. Each arch is a stable tear in the void that opens one way: in. When a soul dies in the living world, it does not appear on Jörmund instantly. It awakens in the grey nothing of Ginnungagap, disoriented, still feeling the echo of its death, a drowning man gasping for air that is already in his lungs, a stabbed woman clutching a wound that is no longer bleeding. The void is dark and silent and unbearably cold. The arches are the only warmth, the only light, the only direction. A soul that has awakened in the void moves toward the nearest arch the way a moth moves toward a flame, not because it understands the destination, but because the light is the only thing worth walking toward.

Some souls awaken already past the arch, on Jörmund's shore, with no memory of the passage at all. The Norns have never explained why this happens to some and not others. Perhaps the void is thinner in places. Perhaps some souls fall through faster than others. The result is the same: the soul arrives on the long shore, whole and disoriented, and begins to walk.

The arches were placed at the four cardinal points because the island's geometry demanded it. The shore curves inward, and the four compass points are the farthest edges of the curve, the places where the membrane between Ginnungagap and Jörmund is thinnest.

The South Arch is the oldest and most worn. More souls have passed through it than any other, because it faces the sea routes where the most people have died throughout history: drownings, shipwrecks, coastal raids. Its stone is salt-bleached and crumbling at the edges, and the runes carved into its columns have been rubbed smooth by the passage of millions of hands reaching out to touch the stone as they stumbled through.

The West Arch faces the volcanic shore and runs hotter than the others. The stone is darker.

The North Arch faces the frozen peaks and is perpetually rimed with frost that does not melt, because the souls who pass through it carry the cold of their dying.

The East Arch is the freshest. Fewer souls have passed through it, for reasons the Norns have never explained.

### 4. The Yggdrasil Seedling

The world-tree on the small western island is a single seedling of Yggdrasil, grown from a cutting the Norns brought when they made Jörmund. Its roots reach down into the Pool, and into the dark beneath it, where something has been gnawing for a very long time. While the Pool flowed, the tree thrived. Now the Pool is dammed and the gnawing has not stopped, and the seedling rots from two directions at once.

In the early game, the seedling is green. It is the only green thing on the island. The dead make pilgrimage to it because it is the only proof they have that life still exists somewhere, that the cycle has not wound down completely, that there is something left worth returning to. As the player progresses and the king's transformation deepens, the seedling visibly declines. Branches yellow. Leaves fall. By the final act, it is a black skeleton against the pale sky, and every falling leaf is a quiet mark of the king's transformation into the thing that eats the roots.

## Part II: The Age of the Open Shore

### 5. When the Cycle Worked

For an immeasurable span, perhaps five thousand years or more by mortal reckoning, the cycle functioned as designed.

A soul would die in the living world. It would awaken in the grey void, or on the shore itself, and find its way to Jörmund. It would arrive disoriented, grieving, often still feeling the pain of its death. The Norns were present in those early ages, not as guides or ushers, but as presences. They did not greet the dead at the arch or explain the rules. They simply existed on the island, going about their work, and the dead, being human, watched them and learned. A newly arrived soul would see an older soul walking toward the center and would ask where they were going. The older soul would say, "To the water." The new soul would ask, "Why?" The older soul would say, "Because it asked me to."

That was the whole of it. The Pool did not call names. It did not speak. What it did was quieter than speech and harder to refuse. A soul that had waited long enough would begin to feel a pull, a gentle pressure behind the eyes, a warmth in the chest that pointed toward the center of the island the way a compass needle points north. The pull was not urgent. It did not command. It simply said, in a voice too low for words: this is what comes next. Most souls followed it. The grief was real, and some souls lingered on the shore for years, decades, even a century or two before the pull grew strong enough to move them. But the pull was patient. It waited. And when the soul was ready, it walked.

The few who resisted the pull found that it moved on. The water did not insist. If a soul stood at the edge and said no, the pull would fade, gently, the way a tide recedes, and the water would turn its attention to the next soul in the queue. The refuser would wait at the back, and the pull would not return for a long time. But it would return, eventually, quieter each time, less like a summons and more like an invitation left open on a table, and most refusers, after a few decades of sitting with their refusal, would feel the pull again and this time follow it.

Permanent refusal was almost unheard of. The shore was a gentle place, and the dead, for the most part, were tired.

During this era, the dead formed communities. Not cities, exactly. Nothing on Jörmund is permanent enough to be a city in the way the living use the word. But clusters of shelters, campfires, gathering places. The dead ate the dim food the island provided. They slept, and sleep was the only true forgetting. They spoke to each other. They told stories. Some of them carved the stories into stone, which is why the player finds carvings scattered across the island: a written record of ten thousand individual griefs, compressed into sentences.

### 6. The Native Creatures

Not everything on Jörmund is a dead soul. The island has its own ecology, sparse and ancient, predating the first human arrival.

The carnifex of the western beaches are the most visible example. They are not souls. They were never alive in the way that humans are alive. They are creatures of the island itself, grown from the same compressed-void stone that Urðr used to carve the shore. Their chitin is the color of old rust because that is the color of Jörmund's bedrock, exposed and hardened by the volcanic heat that bleeds up from beneath the western coast. They have no memory. They have no grief. They simply are, and they will continue to be long after the last soul has passed through the Pool and the island falls silent again.

The bears of the northern forests are similar. Island creatures, not souls. Territorial, not hostile. A bear that charges a trespasser is not attacking. It is enforcing a boundary that has existed for millennia.

The scorpion in the eastern Sundust is perhaps the oldest living thing on Jörmund. It hunts because hunting is the only behavior it knows, and it has been perfecting that behavior for longer than any human civilization has existed.

These creatures remind the player that Jörmund is not a human place. It was not made for the dead. It was made for the passage, and the dead are temporary occupants. The island does not care about them.

## Part III: The Settlements

There are many places on Jörmund where the dead have gathered, but seven have lasted long enough to be called settlements. Three are the towns the player visits. Four are smaller, older, stranger, and not all of them welcome visitors.

### 7. Wickbarrow, the First Hearth

Founded approximately 2,800 years before the present.

Wickbarrow is the oldest continuously inhabited settlement on Jörmund, though the word continuously requires qualification. The population turns over as souls are called to the Pool, and the buildings are rebuilt every few centuries as the old ones crumble. Nothing on Jörmund is truly permanent. But the location of Wickbarrow has been a gathering point for as long as anyone can remember, because it sits at the natural convergence of the paths leading from the South Arch toward the center.

The name is a compound. Wick, from the Old Norse vík, meaning a dwelling or settlement, and barrow, a burial mound. The dead named it for what surrounded it: the barrow-mounds to the south and east, where the earliest arrivals had dug shallow graves for themselves out of habit before realizing that the dead do not need graves. Those mounds still exist. The Skeleton King claimed them, centuries later, as his throne room.

In the Age of the Open Shore, Wickbarrow was where the Norns could most often be found going about their work. Urðr would sit at the edge of the camp and listen to the newly arrived tell the stories of their lives. Verðandi would walk among the dead, checking the weave, ensuring that the black blood ran properly, that the dim food sustained, that the sleep brought its small mercy of forgetting. Skuld passed through rarely, and when she did, she said nothing. She simply walked, and somewhere behind her, a soul would feel the pull.

After the damming, the camp became permanent. The dead who had been waiting for decades or centuries began to build more substantial shelters. Walls went up. A smith opened a forge, not because the dead needed weapons, but because the act of making something was the only antidote to the paralysis of waiting. A bath-house was established, because the smell of black blood intensified as the population grew and the turnover stopped. The notice board appeared, a public surface where the dead could post warnings, requests, and grievances.

Wickbarrow today is a fortified town, not because anyone attacked it, but because walls give the dead the illusion of safety in a place where safety is meaningless. The walls are psychological, not tactical. They say: this is ours, and the wilderness beyond is not.

### 8. Storsprunga, the Sailors' Rest

Founded approximately 1,600 years before the present.

Storsprunga sits on the southwest coast, where the land splits into a series of tidal inlets and rocky shelves. It was founded by the dead who arrived through the West Arch and chose not to walk inward toward the center. Many of them were sailors, fishermen, and coastal people in life. The sea was the last thing they knew, and the sound of water against rock was the only comfort the long shore could offer them. They gathered on the coast and built shelters from driftwood and tidal stone.

The name comes from the geography. A massive geological crack runs through the cliff face behind the town, deep enough to swallow a ship's mast, where the volcanic activity of Brann to the west has fractured the island's bedrock. The crack glows faintly orange on still nights, a reflection of the magma far below, and the dead of Storsprunga have built their lives around it the way the living build around a hearth. It is warm. On an island where nothing changes, warmth is currency.

Storsprunga is smaller than Wickbarrow and less fortified. It has the feeling of a fishing village, not because anyone fishes, but because the dead who live there arrange their homes the way fishing villages are arranged, with docks and net-racks and boat-sheds, all of them empty. It is a community sustained by muscle memory.

### 9. Nasus, the Northern Keep

Founded approximately 1,100 years before the present.

Nasus sits on the south shore of the central lake, a stone-walled keep looking out across the water toward the King's Tower on its island. It was not founded by accident or habit, the way Wickbarrow and Storsprunga were. It was founded with intention.

When the dead who settled the northern forests found that the creatures of Hollowmere, the ferals that had grown old and strong in the deep woods, were becoming too numerous and too aggressive to live alongside, they retreated south toward the central lake. The shoreline offered clear sight-lines across the water, defensible ground, and enough distance from the treeline that a soul could sleep without listening for the sound of something crawling through the undergrowth. They built Nasus as a fortress because the lake demanded a watcher.

The name is a compound. Nás, meaning corpse, and hús, meaning house. Corpse-House. House of the Dead. The name is grim and the dead who live there use it without irony. They know what they are. The northern settlements have fewer illusions than the southern ones, because the north is colder, sparser, and closer to the places where the island's architecture begins to fray. The dead of Nasus have seen what happens when a soul wanders too far north, when the frost takes the fingers and the silence takes the mind. They built walls because walls are the only argument against the dark.

### 10. Hollowmere, the Forest Camp

Founded approximately 1,400 years before the present, abandoned and resettled several times since.

Hollowmere is not a town. It is a camp at the edge of the northern deep forest, a loose collection of shelters and firepit circles that has been abandoned and rebuilt so many times that the ground is layered with the charcoal of a thousand dead campfires. The dead who live there are the ones who chose to stay in the woods rather than retreat to Nasus, either because they arrived from the North Arch and the forest was the first place they found, or because they are stubborn, or because they are already too far along the fraying to make the walk east.

The camp sits in a clearing at the border between the southern forest, which is thin and manageable, and the deep northern woods, which are not. The clearing is maintained by the inhabitants, who cut back the undergrowth every few weeks to keep the treeline from closing in. If they stopped cutting, the forest would swallow Hollowmere in a season. They know this. The cutting is a ritual. It is the thing that separates them from the ferals who live deeper in the trees.

When Gymir stirred in the Hollow Mountain, approximately 1,100 years ago, the dead of Hollowmere felt it first. The ground shook. The trees swayed. A sound came from the mountain, not a roar exactly but a low, sustained groan, the sound of something enormous shifting in its sleep. Most of the camp fled south to what would become Nasus. The ones who stayed are the ancestors of the current inhabitants, and they carry the memory of that night like a scar. They do not speak the giant's name. They call him "the one in the mountain" and they watch the peak with the careful attention of people who know that the thing inside it is awake.

### 11. Brann's Crucible

Founded approximately 1,800 years before the present.

There is a settlement on the volcanic shore, though calling it a settlement stretches the word thin. Brann's Crucible is a ring of stone shelters built into the lava flows west of Storsprunga, where the ground is warm enough to heat water and the air smells of sulfur and old fire. A few dozen dead live there, drawn to the heat the way the sailors of Storsprunga are drawn to the sound of the sea.

The Crucible predates the Cinder Sovereign's claim on Brann by several centuries. When Ása Grimsdóttir arrived and took the volcano as her domain, the inhabitants of the Crucible found themselves living in the territory of something that burned everything it did not recognize. They adapted. They learned the Sovereign's patterns, the routes she walked, the times she slept. They built their shelters in the gaps between her patrols. They do not worship her, but they do not challenge her either. She is a fact of their geography, like the lava vents and the sulfur springs. You do not argue with a volcano.

The dead of the Crucible are some of the hardest souls on the island. They have been living next to a creature that could destroy them at any moment for nearly two thousand years, and the proximity has given them a particular kind of calm, the calm of people who have already accepted the worst thing that could happen and are simply waiting for it to arrive.

### 12. The Ashfield

Founded approximately 600 years before the present.

East of the main path, on the dry plateau overlooking the Sundust, there is a community of dead who have been there long enough to build low stone walls and a communal well that draws nothing but dust. They call the place the Ashfield because the ground is covered in a fine grey powder that might be volcanic ash or might be the ground-down remnants of souls who dissolved here long ago and were never swept away.

The Ashfield is the closest settlement to the three optional bosses in the eastern desert. Its inhabitants are aware of Gullveig, the scorpion, and the worm-mother, and they have developed a culture of avoidance. They do not hunt. They do not explore. They stay within their walls and tend small gardens of the dim food that grows everywhere on Jörmund, and they mark the directions of danger on standing stones at the settlement's edges so that passing travelers know which way not to walk. The standing stones are the Ashfield's only contribution to the broader culture of the dead, and they are excellent. A traveler who reads the stones carefully will know, within a hundred paces, where every lethal creature in the eastern desert sleeps.

### 13. The Driftworks

Founded and abandoned repeatedly, most recently rebuilt approximately 200 years before the present.

On the northern coast, between the North Arch and the frozen peaks, there is a cluster of structures built from the wreckage of boats that never sailed. The dead call it the Driftworks because the materials arrived as driftwood, carried by the tides that lap against Jörmund's northern shore, tides that should not exist in a place where the water does not truly move. The driftwood comes from somewhere. No one knows where. It arrives on the beach in the morning, grey and salt-crusted, and the dead of the Driftworks build with it because there is nothing else to build with in the north.

The Driftworks has been abandoned and resettled more times than anyone can count. The northern coast is brutal. The frost takes the dead's fingers and blackens their skin, and although the dead cannot truly freeze to death, the sensation of freezing is real and unending, and eventually even the most stubborn soul decides that the south is preferable to the pain. But someone always comes back. Someone always rebuilds. The current inhabitants are a mix of the very old, who have been on the island long enough that the cold no longer registers, and the very new, who arrived through the North Arch and have not yet realized they have a choice.

### 14. Kaldrholt, the Cold-Wood

Founded approximately 900 years before the present.

North of the Driftworks, where the coastal flats give way to a band of stunted pines that grow sideways in the wind, there is a settlement built into the trees themselves. Kaldrholt is a place of platforms and rope bridges, shelters lashed to the trunks of trees that have grown so slowly in the cold that their wood is dense as iron. The dead who live there chose the canopy because the ground was worse. The permafrost buckles in the winter, cracking foundations and swallowing posts, and after a few centuries of rebuilding at ground level the inhabitants gave up and moved upward.

The name is plain. Kaldr, cold. Holt, a wood or grove. The dead of Kaldrholt do not romanticize their home. It is cold. There are trees. The trees are the only reason anyone stays, because the canopy breaks the wind, and on an island where the northern gales carry ice crystals sharp enough to score stone, a windbreak is worth more than walls.

Kaldrholt is the last settlement where the dead still practice carpentry in any serious way. The platforms need constant repair. The ropes fray. The trees, which grow a finger's width per century, sometimes split without warning, dropping an entire section of the settlement into the frozen mud below. The carpenters of Kaldrholt have developed a system of joints and bindings that requires no nails, because nails crack in the cold. Everything is lashed, pegged, and wedged. The resulting structures creak in the wind like ships at anchor, and the dead who sleep in them say the sound is almost comforting, if you have been on the island long enough to find comfort in things that refuse to be quiet.

### 15. Frostgard, the Northern Gate

Founded approximately 700 years before the present.

Beyond the tree line, where the pines thin to nothing and the ground becomes bare rock crusted with lichen and ice, there is a walled compound that the dead call Frostgard. It is the northernmost permanent settlement on Jörmund. Nothing lives beyond it except the creatures of the peaks and the silence.

Frostgard was built by the dead who walked north and kept walking, past Hollowmere, past the Driftworks, past the trees, until the ground leveled out into a high plateau and they could see the frozen peaks in every direction. They built walls because the plateau offered nothing else to build with. The stone was everywhere, loose and sharp, broken from the cliffs by centuries of freeze and thaw. They stacked it without mortar. The walls are thick, uneven, and ugly, and they have stood for seven hundred years because the cold that breaks everything else has frozen the stones together more permanently than any mason could.

The name means frost enclosure, and the dead who live there use it the way a soldier uses the name of a fort. Frostgard is not a home. It is a position. The dead who stay there are the ones who feel most strongly that something in the north needs watching, though none of them can articulate exactly what. They patrol the walls. They maintain a fire in the central pit that has not gone out in four centuries. They do not speak often. The cold makes speech painful after a while, the jaw stiffening, the tongue thickening, and the dead of Frostgard have developed a language of gestures and nods that conveys everything they need to say.

The player arrives at Frostgard and finds a community that has stripped itself down to function. There is a smith, because weapons break faster in the cold. There is a merchant, because travelers still pass through on their way to the peaks. There is no bath-house. The dead of Frostgard consider the cold itself a kind of cleansing.

### 16. Vatnborg, the Lake-Fortress

Founded approximately 500 years before the present.

Where the northern plateau slopes downward toward the central lake, the dead built Vatnborg. It is the only settlement on the island that was designed, from its first stone, as a fortress. Not a gathering place that grew walls. Not a camp that hardened into permanence. A fortress, planned and constructed by a dead woman named Sigríðr who had been a jarl's architect in life and who spent her first century on the island drawing plans in the dirt with a stick before anyone would help her build.

The location was chosen for its sight-lines. From Vatnborg's north wall, you can see the frozen peaks. From the south wall, you can see the central lake, and on the lake, the island where the King's Tower stands. The Tower is visible from Vatnborg on clear days, a dark vertical line against the grey water, and the dead of Vatnborg have been watching it for five hundred years. They know the Tower's silhouette the way a sailor knows a coastline. They can tell the weather by the way the Tower's shadow falls on the lake. They have counted the lights that sometimes appear in the Tower's upper windows, and they have theories about what those lights mean, none of which are comforting.

Vatnborg is the last town the player visits before the Crowned Bridge. It has the feeling of a frontier outpost at the edge of a war that has not yet started. The dead who live there are not fighters, mostly. They are watchers. But Sigríðr built armories into the walls, and the armories are full, and the dead of Vatnborg maintain the weapons the way the dead of Kaldrholt maintain their ropes: because maintenance is the only control they have over a situation that is otherwise entirely out of their hands.

The Crowned Bridge is visible from Vatnborg's southern gate. It stretches across the water toward the Tower, a stone causeway that should not be able to support its own weight but does, because the King's herald stands on it and the herald's presence holds the stones together through sheer force of refusal. The dead of Vatnborg do not cross the bridge. They watch it. They have been watching it for five hundred years.

### The Altars of the Northern Path

Scattered along the paths between the northern settlements, there are altars. They are older than any of the towns. They were carved by the earliest dead to walk the north, souls who arrived through the arches in the first centuries after the Pool opened and who marked their passing the way all travelers mark passages: with cairns, with carvings, with a stone set upright in the ground as if to say, I was here, and the silence did not take me.

The Strandvík Altar sits where the mountain path meets the coast, a flat stone slab overlooking a cove where the driftwood collects in patterns that change with the tide. The carvings on its face are so old that they have been worn to shallow grooves by the salt wind, readable only by touch.

The Barrholt Altar stands in the last thick stand of pines before the canopy thins toward Kaldrholt. A black pine has grown through a crack in the altar's base, splitting the stone into two halves that lean against the trunk like pages of an open book. The dead read the carvings on both halves and argue about what the original text said before the tree divided it.

The Vindfell Altar is the highest point on the northern path. It sits on a bare ridge where the wind is constant and the view extends in every direction. On clear days you can see the smoke from Brann's volcano to the southwest, the dark line of the deep forest below, and the frozen peaks to the north. The altar is anchored to the ridge with iron pins driven into the rock, because without them the wind would have taken it centuries ago.

The Ísvarða Altar is a cairn, not a carved slab. It stands at the border between the plateau and the lake-slope, a stack of stones taller than a man, each stone placed by a different hand over the course of a thousand years. The newest stones are at the top, smooth and pale. The oldest are at the bottom, dark with lichen and fused to the bedrock by centuries of ice.

The Vatnsteinn Altar is the last altar before the Crowned Bridge. It sits on the lakeshore, half-submerged at high water, fully exposed at low. The carvings on its face record the names of the dead who walked past it on their way to the Pool, back when the Pool was still accepting. The list is long. The most recent name was carved over three hundred years ago.

The Kolfjall Altar marks the fork where the northern path splits toward the eastern plateau and the Ashfield beyond. The stone is dark, almost black, and the dead say it was quarried from the base of a peak that burned in some geological event so old that even the island's stones have forgotten it. The carvings on the Kolfjall Altar are not names or stories. They are directions. They tell the traveler which way is safe and which way is not, and they are, by all accounts, accurate.

## Part IV: The Three Who Weave (and the One Who Stopped)

### 17. Urðr, She Who Remembers

Urðr is the eldest of the three. Her name means fate, or what has become, and her domain is the past. Every thread that has been woven, every life that has been lived, every moment that has passed. She is the keeper of the complete record. If you imagine the entirety of existence as a tapestry, Urðr can see every thread from its first twist to its current position. She forgets nothing. She loses nothing. The weight of this is incalculable.

In the Age of the Open Shore, Urðr was the most visible of the three Norns. She sat with the dead and listened. Not because she needed to hear their stories, she already knew them, but because the act of telling was part of the passage. A soul that speaks its life aloud to someone who truly listens is a soul that begins to release its grip on what it was. Urðr was the first step in letting go.

When the cycle broke, Urðr retreated to her loom. She is still there, somewhere deep in the island, in a place the dead have not found, a chamber carved before the arches were shaped, where the threads of every past life are stored on spindles of void-bone. She weaves still, because the past does not stop accumulating just because the future has. Every moment the dead experience on the stagnant shore is a new thread of what was, and Urðr must weave it. She is drowning in threads. The tapestry has become so dense, so tangled with the accumulated present-becoming-past of thousands of trapped souls, that she can no longer see the patterns she once read with ease. She has gone quiet not because she is hiding, but because she has nothing to say. The past is too loud. It has swallowed her voice.

### 18. Verðandi, She Who Is

Verðandi is the middle sister. Her name means becoming, or what is happening, and her domain is the present moment. She does not remember and she does not foresee. She sustains. Every soul that exists on Jörmund right now exists because Verðandi's loom is running. If she stopped weaving, even for a heartbeat, the dead would unravel. Their black blood would thin to mist, their forms would blur, their memories would dissolve faster than they already do. She is, in a very real sense, holding the island together by working her loom every day.

This is why she cannot leave Wickbarrow. This is why she cannot intervene in the king's tyranny, or guide the player directly, or do anything more than sit in her workshop and read the runes for anyone who asks. She is load-bearing. The island rests on her shoulders, and if she sets it down to fight, the island falls.

She presents to the dead as the local fortune teller. She does not advertise her true nature. The dead who visit her believe she is an unusually gifted mortal seer, a woman who arrived on the shore with the gift of rune-sight and has been reading the present for paying customers ever since. She encourages this belief. The truth, that she is one of the three weavers who built the island, who wove the substance of every soul on it, who can see the thread of every life that ever touched the shore, would not help anyone. It would make them afraid, and the dead are already afraid enough.

She names herself to the player, once, quietly, late in the conversation, because the player is the first living soul she has spoken to since the Norns stopped walking openly, and the sound of her own name in a room where someone is still breathing is a mercy she cannot refuse.

### 19. Skuld, She Who Hides

Skuld is the youngest sister, and she is the absent god of this story.

Her name means debt, or what shall be, and her domain is the future. Every thread that has not yet been woven, every life that has not yet been lived, every birth that has not yet happened. In the Age of the Open Shore, Skuld was the quietest of the three. She did not sit with the dead. She did not walk among them. She simply existed somewhere on the island, doing her work, and the work expressed itself as the pull: the gentle pressure behind the eyes that told a soul its time had come. Skuld did not call names. She did not speak at all. She wove the next life on her loom, and when the weaving was ready, the pull reached the soul it was meant for, and the soul walked to the Pool.

When Hjarrandi stood at the water's edge and turned his back, the pull faded. The water did not insist. It moved on to the next soul. But Hjarrandi's followers were blocking the paths to the Pool, and the next soul could not reach it, and the one after that could not reach it, and the backlog grew until Skuld's loom went dark. She could not weave the next life because the next life depended on a soul stepping into the water, and no soul was stepping in. The future she was meant to weave had become entirely contingent on the resolution of a single man's refusal, and that man had said no.

With no future left to weave, Skuld went into the deep places of the island, the folds of Ginnungagap beneath the shore, and she waited. She is still waiting. She has been waiting for four hundred years.

The act of pulling the player down to the long shore was her last intervention. She reached across the threshold, a thing only she could do because she is the weaver of what shall be, and the player's arrival on Jörmund is a future event she is choosing to make happen. It cost her. The Norns are not meant to intervene directly. They are meant to weave and let the weave play out. By pulling the player, Skuld broke her own rule, and the breaking left a mark on her. A frayed edge in the fabric of the island, a place where the membrane between Jörmund and the void is thinner than it should be. This is the sign she left on the western seedling-isle, not a message, not a symbol, but a wound in the weave, visible only to someone who knows what to look for. Verðandi can tell the player what to look for. The player must go and see it for themselves.

## Part V: The Damming of the Pool

### 20. Hjarrandi Eylifsson, the Full History

Born approximately 480 years before the present, in the living world.

Hjarrandi was the second son of Eylif Hjarrandison, a minor coastal jarl who controlled a stretch of shoreline between two river-mouths in what is now western Norway. The name Hjarrandi means screamer. It is one of Odin's heiti, a title-name for the Allfather, and Eylif gave it to his son in the hope that the boy would carry the god's voice. This is a common pattern in Norse naming. Parents gave their children the names of gods not out of hubris but out of prayer. To name your son Hjarrandi was to say: let the Allfather see this child and favor him.

Eylif died in his bed. Peacefully, in his sleep, without a weapon in his hand. By the standards of his world, this was a shameful death. A man who did not die fighting would not be carried to the hall of the slain. Hjarrandi, who was seventeen when his father died, felt two things at once: grief for a father he had loved, and a hot, bitter contempt for the manner of his going. He swore, in the way that young men swear, that he would not die like that. He would die with a sword in his hand and blood on his face and the sound of his name in the mouths of his enemies.

He inherited the jarldom and immediately began what would become a forty-year campaign of unification. He was not a conqueror in the usual sense. He did not burn villages or enslave populations. He arrived at each neighboring kingdom's hall with a warband at his back and a speech on his lips, and he offered terms: submit, join, and prosper under a single law, or fight and lose everything. Most chose to submit. The seven who resisted were defeated in battle, but Hjarrandi treated the defeated jarls with uncommon generosity. He let them keep their halls, their titles, their lands. He took only their loyalty. By the time he was sixty, the seven coastal kingdoms were one, and Hjarrandi Eylifsson was king of a shoreline that stretched from the southern fjords to the northern ice.

He ruled well. This is the detail that makes him a tragedy rather than a villain. He was just. He was generous at feast. He settled disputes with a fairness that surprised even his critics. He built roads, established trade routes, and defended his coast against raiders from the east. His people loved him. His jarls respected him.

He died at eighty. In his bed. In his sleep. Without a weapon in his hand.

He died exactly the way his father had died, the death he had sworn he would never share, and the last thing he felt before the darkness took him was a shame so deep it reached the marrow. The great unifier, the screamer, the man who had spent sixty years building a kingdom so that his name would echo through the ages, had ended exactly like the coward he pitied. He did not even wake up to fight it.

He arrived on Jörmund through the South Arch, the farthest point from the Pool, and he was not content. He was quiet. The dead who saw him in those first weeks describe a tall man walking slowly, looking at everything, speaking to no one. He did not grieve the way the newly arrived usually grieve. He did not weep for the life he had left. He did not clutch at the phantom wound of his death, because there was no wound. He had simply stopped, the way a candle stops, and the absence of a final wound was the wound itself.

The walk from the South Arch to the Pool is long. The island is wide. A soul walking at the pace of the dead, which is the pace of people who have nowhere to be, might take months to reach the center. Hjarrandi took years.

He did not take years because he was slow. He took years because he found, along the way, the same thing he had found in life: people who needed organizing. The dead of the southern settlements were scattered, aimless, shuffling through their centuries without purpose or direction. Hjarrandi saw them and he saw what he had always seen, a people who needed a voice, a structure, a reason to stand up in the morning. He began to do what he did best. He spoke to them. He gathered them. He settled disputes between camps. He helped build a wall here, a shelter there, a path between two places that had been isolated from each other by undergrowth. He was a king again, not by title but by habit, and the habit felt better than anything else on the long shore.

By the time he reached the central lake, he had walked the entire breadth of the island and left a trail of small improvements behind him. He had allies. He had people who owed him favors. He had a reputation, the man who fixed things. He stood at the water's edge and he felt the pull, the quiet tug behind the eyes that said this is what comes next, and for the first time in his long walk, he stopped moving.

The pull asked him to step in. To be unmade. To let go of his name and his memory and his kingdom and his shame and his sixty years of work and be returned to the living world as a blank thing, a newborn with no history, no accomplishments, no crown. He thought of his father, dying in his sleep. He thought of himself, dying in his sleep. He thought of the dead he had helped along the way, the ones who called him king without being asked. He thought of the water, still and patient, and what it would make of him: nothing. A fresh start. A fisherman's child or a goatherd or a thrall.

He said no.

The pull faded. The water did not insist. It turned its attention to the next soul, and Hjarrandi stepped back from the edge and looked at the island he had crossed and decided that he would not be unmade. He would stay. He would rule. He would be what he had been in life, because being what he had been in life was the only thing that kept the shame at bay. If he stopped being a king, he would have to be a man who died in his sleep, and that man was unbearable.

The first followers came easily. He did not need to recruit. He simply continued doing what he had been doing on the walk: helping, organizing, speaking. The dead who had followed him before followed him now, and new arrivals, hearing his name at the South Arch, walked toward the center with the assumption that the man at the lake was in charge. He told them about the Pool. He told them what it would do. He told them they had a choice. Not everyone stayed. Many still felt the pull and followed it. But enough stayed, and enough is all a king needs.

The tower was built over a century and a half. Four hundred years of builders, masons, carvers, and laborers who do not tire, do not sleep unless they choose to, and have nothing else to fill their time. The result is not a crude stack of stone. It is immaculate. The tower rises from the center island like a crown of dark rock, its walls fitted so precisely that a knife blade cannot find the seam between stones. The interior is carved with geometric patterns that the dead brought from their living memories, Norse knotwork and spiral motifs and runic inscriptions that no longer mean anything but look like they should. There are halls inside the tower, and stairs, and a throne room at the top where the king sits beneath a ceiling so high it disappears into shadow. The throne itself is carved from a single block of the island's dark stone, polished until it reflects the pale sky like a mirror. It is beautiful. It is the most beautiful thing on the island, and every stone of it was laid by a soul who chose to build rather than walk into the water.

### 21. The Court in the Tower

Hjarrandi's inner circle is small. By the time the player arrives, most of his original followers have either gone feral, been destroyed by previous champions the Norns sent, or quietly slipped away to hide in the farthest corners of the island. The court that remains consists of the bridge-keeper, the lord of Sprekken, and the unnamed enforcers.

Heimdall's Echo, the bridge-keeper, was one of Hjarrandi's housecarls in life, a member of the personal guard that protected the king in his living hall. His name has been lost. Not forgotten. Given away. In life, he was a quiet man. He was not the best fighter in the warband, not the most charismatic, not the most ambitious. He was loyal. He was patient. He was the man who stood behind the king's chair during every feast, who slept across the king's door every night, who followed the king into every battle and stood at his shoulder and did whatever was needed. He had a name. It does not matter.

He arrived through the South Arch approximately 350 years before the present, fifty years after Hjarrandi. He walked to the tower, found his king, and knelt. Hjarrandi was surprised to see him. They had not spoken in life. The king barely knew his face. But the housecarl did not need to be known. He needed to serve. That was the shape of his self, and the shape held.

When Hjarrandi asked for a bridge-keeper, the housecarl volunteered. Hjarrandi asked his name. The housecarl said, "I have no name. I am your echo." Hjarrandi gave him the horn and the title, and the man walked to the bridge and has not left it since.

The Skeleton King, whose name in life was Bjálfi Ketilsson, is the lord of Sprekken. He arrived three centuries before Hjarrandi and was the first soul to refuse the Pool. When Hjarrandi arrived and built the tower, Bjálfi looked at the larger king and felt a flicker of the same thing he had felt when his brother came with the warband in life: the recognition that someone else was doing what he had done, but better. He knelt. Hjarrandi gave him Sprekken to guard, and he has guarded it for three hundred years.

The unnamed enforcers patrol the paths between the towns, wearing ill-fitting armor taken from the tower's stores. They are not named because they are not individuals anymore. They gave up their names willingly, at Hjarrandi's request, because the king needed soldiers and soldiers do not need names. They are what happens to a soul that surrenders its identity to a cause. They become the cause, hollow and tireless, swinging swords they never learned to use because the king told them to swing. They are dressed up. The wrongness is visible. The armor does not fit. The swords are held badly. The helms are too large. They are children playing at soldiers, and they have been playing for so long they have forgotten it is a game.

### 22. The Transformation

The transformation of Hjarrandi into Níðhöggr is not a sudden event. It is a slow process that has been happening for approximately two hundred years.

In Norse mythology, Fáfnir was a dwarf who killed his father for a hoard of gold and lay on it until the gold's curse transformed him into a dragon. The hoard reshaped the hoarder. This is the template for Hjarrandi's transformation, but the hoard is not gold. It is eternity. Hjarrandi has hoarded time itself, refused to let it pass through him, refused to let the cycle carry him forward, and the hoarding is reshaping him into the thing that gnaws at the root of the world-tree.

The physical changes began small. His fingernails thickened. His skin grew rough. His eyes, which had been the warm brown of a man who spent his life looking at the sea, turned pale and then gold and then slit-pupiled. He noticed. He hid it. He wore gloves, and then long sleeves, and then the heavy silk robes that cover him from throat to floor. The silk is the only concession to vanity he has ever made. In life he wore furs and leather and mail. The silk is as foreign to him as the scales beneath it.

The scales came next. First along the spine, then spreading outward across the ribs, the shoulders, the thighs. They are the color of deep water, blue-black, iridescent, catching light that should not exist in Jörmund's pale sky. They are beautiful, in the way that all terrible things are beautiful. Hjarrandi does not look at them. He does not acknowledge them. When a scale falls from his body, and they do, shedding the way a snake sheds, he has a servant sweep it up and dispose of it in the lake. The dead who find them, caught on briars, in torn silk, in the silt, know what they mean, but no one speaks of it, because speaking of it would mean acknowledging that the king they serve is becoming a monster, and acknowledging that would mean admitting they chose to serve a monster, and admitting that would mean confronting the question of why they chose it, and that question leads to the Pool, and the Pool is exactly what they are all trying to avoid.

Níðhöggr in the old stories is the serpent that gnaws Yggdrasil's roots. Here, the gnawing is literal. The Yggdrasil-seedling on the western island is dying because its roots reach down into the Pool, and beneath the Pool, in the deep dark, something is chewing. Hjarrandi does not know he is Níðhöggr. He does not experience the transformation as a monstrous rebirth. He experiences it as a slow loss of control, a body that moves wrong, a neck that is too long, a jaw that aches. He is frightened. He has been frightened for two hundred years. And the fear is making it worse, because fear is a kind of hoarding too, hoarding the self against the threat of dissolution, and every act of hoarding feeds the change.

There is an irony in this that the player may not see until the final fight. Hjarrandi refused the Pool because he could not bear to be unmade. But the hoarding is unmaking him anyway, just slower, and into something worse. The Pool would have returned him as a child. The hoarding is returning him as a worm. He chose the only option that was not a choice at all, and now the serpent is eating him from the inside, and the roots of the world-tree are dying, and the sky is still, and the dead are waiting.

## Part VI: The Souls Who Twisted

### 23. Boss Backstories

What follows is the complete life-and-death-and-afterlife history of every named vassal, titan, and challenger on Jörmund, organized in the order the player encounters them.

---

The Skeleton King. Bjálfi Ketilsson.

In life, Bjálfi was born the third son of a petty jarl in a river valley east of modern Trondheim, approximately 700 years before the present. The third son of a jarl inherits nothing. The eldest gets the hall, the second gets the warband, the third gets a horse and a direction to ride. Bjálfi rode east. He found a valley no one had claimed, built a longhouse, attracted settlers, and declared himself king of a territory roughly the size of a large farm. His kingdom lasted eleven years. His brother, the second son, came with the warband and disputed the claim. Bjálfi died in the doorway of his own longhouse, defending a throne that was really just a chair with animal pelts draped over it.

On Jörmund, he arrived through the South Arch angry and humiliated. He had been a king for eleven years, and in death, he was nothing. He was called within a decade. He refused. Not because he had a philosophy of refusal. Not because he feared the unmade. He refused because his kingdom had been taken from him by his brother, and the idea of giving up the title of king, the only thing his brother could not take, was more than he could bear.

He built the barrow-mound south of Wickbarrow in imitation of the royal burials of his homeland. He filled it with bones, his own shed substance and the remains of dead who had dissolved too far to reform, and he sat in the center of it, on a chair made of femurs, wearing his iron crown. For three hundred years, he sat there alone, being a king of bones. He was the first refuser. He was what Hjarrandi would have been if Hjarrandi were stupid. He is a foreshadow.

When Hjarrandi arrived and built a tower, Bjálfi knelt. He knelt because kneeling to a greater king was the pattern of his life, and patterns are the last thing a soul loses before it goes feral. His bones are all that remain. His flesh dissolved centuries ago. The crown on his skull is the one he wore in life. It is made of iron, not gold, because Bjálfi was the kind of king who valued function over display. It is the only thing on his body that is still recognizably his.

Corruption: he held the mortal name too tightly.

---

Galgi the Bound. The Gallows-Knot.

Galgi is not one soul. Galgi is several, and the lives they lived are lost. What the dead of Wickbarrow remember is this: approximately 500 years before the present, a group of newly arrived souls, perhaps seven, perhaps nine, the accounts vary, gathered in the sunken grove south of the town and spoke together about the Pool. They had all felt the pull. They had all walked to the water's edge. They had all stood there, looking at the still surface, and felt the terror of being unmade.

One of them suggested a solution. If they tied themselves together, bound at the wrist and throat and ankle, they could not be separated. The Pool's pull reaches a single soul. A knotted mass of souls, fused and tangled, could never be pulled individually, and the water could never ask a question that seven mouths must answer at once. They would be safe. They would be together. They would never be alone in the dark.

They hanged themselves from the gallows-tree in the grove, not to die, they were already dead, but to seal the binding. The rope pulled tight. The bodies fused. The seven or nine became one, and the one was a horror: a tangled mass of limbs and rope and black blood, screaming from several mouths at once, alive in the sense that the dead are alive but no longer individual, no longer separable, no longer able to change its mind. The pull reached each of their names, one by one, over the following century. None of them could answer. The knot held.

Galgi is Old Norse for gallows. The dead of Wickbarrow gave the knot this name the way you name a storm, not out of affection, but out of the need to have a word for the thing that frightens you.

Corruption: they held the mortal name too tightly, the collective variant. They held onto their togetherness as a substitute for individual identity.

---

The Drowned Crone. Rán's Acolyte. Sigrún Brynjadóttir.

In life, her name was Sigrún, and she was a priestess of the sea-goddess Rán in a fishing village on the western coast of Iceland. She was not a powerful priestess. She did not command storms or summon waves. She kept the shrine, tended the net-offerings, and spoke the prayers that kept the fishermen safe when they sailed. She died by drowning. A storm took her from the shore while she was hauling in the offering-nets, and the sea pulled her under, and she did not come back up.

On Jörmund, she arrived through the West Arch soaking wet, which is impossible. The dead do not carry the physical conditions of their deaths with them, except as sensory memory. But Sigrún's devotion to the sea was so complete that her soul remembered the water as vividly as it remembered anything else. She walked to the western coast, waded into the shallows, and began to cast her net.

She has not stopped.

The net is not a physical object. It is a manifestation of her devotion, woven from the same substance as the black blood, memory given form. She casts it into the shallows and it catches things: bones, driftwood, the dissolving fragments of dead souls who wandered too close. She drags the catch to shore, examines it, and throws the net again. She is looking for sailors. In life, her net-offerings were meant to appease Rán so that the fishermen would return safely. In death, she is still trying to bring them in, still trying to pull the drowned from the water, still serving a goddess who cannot hear her because the gods cannot enter Jörmund.

The water in the salt marshes rises when she is angry, which is always, because the net never catches what she is looking for. The deeper the water, the stronger her pull. She has flooded the entire marsh over the centuries, turning a stretch of tidal coast into a permanent shallow lake.

Corruption: she hoarded a domain. The net, the sea, the act of catching.

---

The Cinder Sovereign. Múspellseyja. Ása Grimsdóttir.

In life, her name was Ása, and she was a charcoal-burner's daughter in the volcanic highlands of Iceland. She lived near the fire. She died in it. A fissure eruption consumed her village, and the lava took her while she was trying to carry her father's tools to safety. She was seventeen.

On Jörmund, she arrived through the West Arch and walked straight to Brann, the volcanic shore, because the heat felt like home. She did not walk toward the center. She did not wait for the pull. She did not speak to the other dead. She simply found the hottest place on the island and sat down in it.

The fire did not destroy her. It became her. Over the centuries, the volcanic rock crusted around her body, forming the armor she wears. The flames that crown her are not an attack. They are her hair, transformed by the heat into something that burns without consuming. She guards the western approach not because anyone told her to, she predates Hjarrandi's arrival by two thousand years, but because the fire is hers and she will not share it.

She is one of the oldest souls on the island. She has been at Brann longer than Wickbarrow has existed. The dead of Storsprunga tell stories about her the way the living tell stories about mountains. She is a fact of the landscape, immovable and ancient. She does not serve the king. She does not know the king exists, and if she did, she would not care. She is fire, and fire does not negotiate.

Corruption: she hoarded a domain. Fire, heat, the volcanic shore.

---

Sindri's Apprentice. Höðr.

In life, his name was Höðr, and he was not the son of Sindri, though he called himself so. He was the apprentice of a mastersmith in a mountain village in the Hardanger region of Norway, a man named Gorm who claimed descent from the dwarven smiths of legend but was, in truth, a very talented mortal. Höðr was fourteen when Gorm took him on. He was sixteen when Gorm set him the task of forging a blade, not a weapon, but a blade, a piece of metal so perfectly tempered, so flawlessly shaped, that it would prove the apprentice worthy of the master's craft. Höðr worked the blade for two years. He was eighteen when the forge-fire caught his workshop and burned him alive. The blade was on the anvil when he died. It was not finished.

On Jörmund, he arrived through the West Arch and walked inland, toward the mountains, because the mountains were where the forges were. He found the abandoned dwarven forge past Brann and lit it.

He has been hammering ever since. The blade on the anvil is not the blade from his life. It is a memory of a blade, a shape made from the island's dark metal, and it will not hold. The quench does not set. The temper does not take. The metal remembers nothing, because the metal is made of the same void-substance as everything else on Jörmund, and void-substance does not respond to craft the way iron does. But Höðr does not notice. He sees the blade. He sees the flaw. He heats it, hammers it, quenches it, and the flaw returns. He has been repeating this cycle for three hundred years.

He calls himself Sindri's son because Gorm told him the lineage, and the lineage, true or false, is the only thing he has left of the master he loved. The name is a prayer. I am Sindri's apprentice, therefore I can finish the work. He cannot. He will never finish. But stopping would mean admitting that the blade is nothing, that the craft was nothing, that the two years of labor before the fire took him were nothing, and that admission would dissolve him faster than the Pool.

Corruption: he held the mortal name too tightly. The unfinished work is the name he refuses to release.

---

Gymir of the Hollow Mountain.

Gymir was not human. He was a jötunn, a frost-giant, one of the ancient beings who predated the gods the way the Norns predate them. In the old stories, Gymir is the father of Gerðr, the giantess whom Freyr loved. What is known of the Jörmund Gymir is simpler and sadder. He was a keeper of cold, a being whose purpose was to hold the frost in check, to sleep in the deep stone and keep the ice from spreading. He was placed in the mountain before the arches were shaped, hammered into the stone by forces older than the dead can name, and sealed with runes that were meant to hold until the end of days.

He slept. He slept for thousands of years, through the entire Age of the Open Shore, through the founding of every settlement, through the arrival of Hjarrandi, through the damming of the Pool. He slept through all of it.

He woke because the cycle broke. The threshold's equilibrium depended on the flow of souls through the Pool. When the flow stopped, the structure began to degrade, and the degradation reached the mountain, and the runes that held Gymir weakened. He did not wake suddenly. He woke the way a glacier melts, slowly, one layer at a time. By the time the player arrives, he has been fully awake for approximately three hundred years. He has spent those three hundred years lying in the dark, alone, in a hollow chamber inside the mountain, unable to sleep, unable to leave, unable to do anything except rage at the silence.

The hollow in his chest, the void the player sees through when the boss cracks open, is not a wound. It is what happens to a creature of cold when the cold has nowhere to go. He is empty inside because the frost that filled him has bled away into the stone.

He does not want to fight. He wants to sleep. He cannot. The rage comes from that, and it is bottomless.

Corruption: he hoarded a domain. The mountain, the cold, the sleep he cannot have.

---

Skadi the Hoarfrost. Frída Arnbjörnsdóttir.

In life, her name was Frída, and she was born in a hunting village in the mountains of northern Sweden. Her parents named her for a grandmother, not a goddess. She was an unremarkable child, quiet, watchful, good with a bow but not exceptional. She died on a hunt at sixteen. She was tracking a lynx through fresh snow when the ridge she was crossing gave way. She fell and broke her neck on the rocks below. The last thing she saw was the sky, very blue, and the snow, very white, and a bird circling above her. She was dead before the bird landed.

On Jörmund, she arrived through the North Arch. A child, frightened, alone, with snow still melting in her memory. The pull found her within a decade. She refused. She refused not because she feared the unmade, she was too young to understand what the Pool would do, but because she did not want to stop being Frída. She had only been Frída for sixteen years. She had not finished being herself. She wanted more time.

The more time she got, the more she needed. She discovered, in the decades after her refusal, that the frost on Jörmund could be shaped. The cold here is not natural cold. It is the memory of cold, carried by the souls who died in winter, and Frída, who had died in the coldest winter of her short life, carried more of it than most. She learned to push the cold outward, to form it into ice, to make the frost do what she wanted. It was the first time in either of her lives that she had been good at anything, and it consumed her.

She took the name Skadi approximately a century after her arrival, when a passing soul told her the old stories and she recognized herself, or thought she did. A goddess of the hunt, of the mountains, of the frost. It was not vanity. It was aspiration. She wanted to be Skadi because Skadi was everything Frída had been too small, too young, too ordinary to become. The name was a shield against her own insignificance.

Two centuries later, the shield has become a prison. She is fast, cold, and precise, everything Skadi should be, but beneath the persona, Frída is still there, still sixteen, still watching the bird circle above her broken body. The ice around her is not natural. She made it. She made all of it, the frost and the wind and the persona, because the alternative was admitting she was just a dead girl on a shore with nowhere to go.

She is the youngest boss on the island, and the one who has been lying to herself the longest.

Corruption: she took a god's name as a shield.

---

Hrímfaxi's Get. The Wolf-Mother.

In life, she was not a wolf. She was a woman, a herder, a mother, a person with a name that has been lost. She arrived through the North Arch so long ago that no one remembers when. She is older than Nasus, older than the founding of Storsprunga, possibly older than Wickbarrow. She is one of the most ancient human souls on the island.

The pull found her. She turned away from it. The refusal was not ideological. It was maternal. She had children in the living world, three or four or five, the number has blurred, and the idea of being unmade and returned as an infant with no memory of the children she had raised was a violence she could not accept. She would wait. She would wait until her children arrived through the arches, and she would walk into the Pool with them, and they would be unmade together, and whatever they became in the next life would carry a trace of the bond they had shared.

Her children did arrive, one by one, over the following decades. She was there at the arch each time. She held them. She wept. She told them the plan. They agreed. They waited.

The pull found the eldest first. The eldest walked in alone. The mother screamed, but the pull does not wait for screams. It asks, and the soul follows, and the following is final. The second child was called a year later. The second walked in too. The third. The fourth. Each one, when the moment came, chose the Pool over the mother, because the pull is gentle and the future it promises is new, and the children, who were younger than the mother and less burdened by the weight of what they were leaving behind, chose the new.

She was alone. She was alone and she had refused the Pool for the sake of children who chose the Pool without her. The grief broke her. She did not go feral in the usual way. She did not forget her name or lose her language or drop to all fours. She chose to drop to all fours. She chose to become a thing that protects its young, and the island, which shapes the forgotten into what it needs, gave her the form she wanted: four legs, grey fur, a jaw full of teeth, and a howl that freezes the blood. The pups she raises are not her children. They are feral dead, souls that lost themselves in the northern woods, that she has adopted and nursed and shaped into something that resembles a pack. She feeds them on the corpses of lost pilgrims because that is what a mother does. She feeds her young, no matter the cost.

She is protecting something. What she is protecting is the idea of a family that abandoned her. There is nothing in the den but old bones and claw-marks on the stone, and the claw-marks are in the shape of names she can no longer read.

Corruption: she hoarded a domain. Motherhood, protection, the pack.

---

The Ash-Sprout. A Bough of Yggdrasil.

The Ash-Sprout was never alive in the human sense. It is a bough, a branch of the Yggdrasil-seedling on the western island, which is itself a cutting grown from the true world-tree. When the cycle broke and the seedling began to die, one of its branches fell. Branches of Yggdrasil do not fall passively. They fall into, seeking soil, seeking root, trying to survive the way a cutting tries to survive when it is separated from the parent plant. The bough fell into the northern caldera, far from its mother-tree, and began to grow.

It is angry and lonely and the seed in its chest still remembers what it was meant to grow into: a world-tree, a pillar of the cosmos, a living axis connecting all the worlds. Instead, it is a stunted, blackened thing in a volcanic depression, fed by corruption rather than the Pool's flow, rotting from the inside out while simultaneously, desperately, trying to grow. The corruption, the black sap it sprays during combat, is the discharge of failed growth. It is trying to root and the soil will not take it. It is trying to branch and the air will not hold it.

The seed in its chest, the glowing core the player must target in phase two, is the part that remembers. It is the only part of the Ash-Sprout that is still connected to the original seedling. Destroying it is an act of mercy and an act of murder. The seed dies, and the bough stops suffering, but the seedling on the western island loses another thread of connection to the tree it grew from.

This is the only boss that does not fit cleanly into the three categories. It is not a soul that refused the Pool. It is a piece of the island's architecture that broke. It is the closest thing the game has to a natural disaster with a health bar.

---

Eir of Broken Wings. The Fallen Valkyrie.

Eir is not a mortal soul. She is something older, winged, half-divine, bound to a purpose she can no longer fulfill. Her name means mercy or protection, and those who remember her on the island say she was gentler than anything that powerful had a right to be. She carried the dying softly, spoke to them in voices that eased the fear, and delivered them to the threshold with care.

How she came to be inside Jörmund is a question the dead argue about. The arches open one way. Nothing that is not a dead soul should be able to pass through. And yet Eir is here, grounded, wingless, raging in a fortress she did not build. The most common story, told by the oldest dead in Nasus, is that she was mid-flight when the cycle broke, carrying a soul through the North Arch, and the arch stuttered, just for an instant. The one-way rule flickered. She was pulled through with the soul she carried. The arch closed behind her. Her wings cracked against the lintel as it sealed. She fell.

The fortress she inhabits in the northeast was built by the dead, centuries before, as a waystation for souls walking toward the center. Eir crashed into it the way a bird crashes into a window. She cannot leave. She cannot die. She cannot fly. Her wings, which should heal, cannot heal, because whatever sustains her cannot be replenished from inside the threshold. She is cut off from everything that made her what she was, and the result is a slow degradation, not the black-blood feral degradation of the dead, but a different kind: a loss of purpose. She paces the halls and fights everything that enters because fighting is the only function she has left, and performing it, even badly, even cruelly, is better than stopping and feeling the absence of everything else.

The revive mechanic in her fight is her nature surfacing. She dies, and something pulls her back together, because she was not made to stay dead. But each revival is weaker. She screams when it happens because the revival hurts, not physically, but in the way that being forced to continue a life you can no longer live hurts. She does not want to be restored. She wants to fly. She cannot.

Corruption: she hoarded a domain. Flight, purpose, the identity she cannot release.

---

Heimdall's Echo. Name Lost.

His full backstory is recorded in the section on the court in the tower. He was a housecarl. He was loyal. He gave away his name. He took a god's title and a horn and a bridge and made himself a monument to someone else's ambition. He is very, very good at fighting. He has had nothing else to do.

Corruption: he took a god's name as a shield.

---

Gullveig Thrice-Burned. Optional.

Gullveig is ancient. She is one of the four oldest souls on Jörmund, older than Hjarrandi by at least a thousand years. In the old stories told by the living, Gullveig was a witch the gods tried to kill three times by burning her. She returned each time. The poets thought she was Freyja in disguise. They were wrong. She is herself, Gullveig, which means gold-drink or gold-power, a name she carried in life as a völva who specialized in seiðr-magic and the transmutation of base metals. She was burned by her own village when they decided she was cursed. She came back. They burned her again. She came back. The third time, they burned the whole village to make sure. She came back, and the village was gone, and she had nothing to come back to.

On Jörmund, she arrived through the East Arch and walked to the plateau where she now stands. The pull found her. She refused, because refusing is what she does. She was burned three times in life and she survived. The Pool would be a fourth fire, and she will not burn again.

Each time she is killed in combat, she returns. Not through the arch-reformation process that the ordinary dead use. She returns in place, in the same arena, wreathed in new fire, angrier than before. She has done this so many times that the ash on the plateau is layered in three distinct bands of soot. By her third return, the third round of her boss fight, she is golden, levitating, beautiful, the version of herself she was always trying to become, a woman so thoroughly defined by survival that she has become survival itself.

She does not know why she keeps coming back. She does not know how to stop. She has forgotten the names of the people who burned her, the village she lived in, the seiðr she practiced. All she remembers is the fire, and the return, and the fire again.

---

The Howling Hollow. Garmr's Cousin. Optional.

The Howling Hollow is not a dead soul. It is a creature of Jörmund itself, one of the native animals, like the carnifex and the bears, but older and stranger. It resembles a hound, enormous, with a hollow chest cavity through which the wind blows, producing the howl that gives it its name. The dead call it Garmr's Cousin because Garmr in the old stories was the great hound who guarded the gate of the dead, and this creature guards the cave on the east coast with the same tireless vigilance.

It is not Garmr. It is something simpler and older: a guardian placed at the cave mouth by the island's own logic. What it guards is unclear. The cave leads deeper into the island's stone, toward the folds of Ginnungagap that the Norns used as raw material, and perhaps the Hollow is simply preventing anything from climbing up from the void. It does not eat. It does not sleep. It howls, and the howl carries a quality of loss so profound that it disrupts the coherence of anything that hears it.

---

The Sea-Mire. Spawn of Jörmungandr. Optional.

Jörmungandr is the World Serpent. It coils around Midgard at the boundary of the void. The Sea-Mire is not Jörmungandr. It is a spawn, a fragment, a child-serpent that fell to Jörmund when the cycle broke.

When the cycle broke, the structural integrity of the threshold degraded. The folds of Ginnungagap that separate Jörmund from the other worlds thinned. The World Serpent, which coils at the boundary of the void, was pressed against the thinned membrane, and something leaked through. Not the serpent itself, but a shed fragment, a scale, a piece of cast-off skin that carried enough of the serpent's essence to grow. It fell into the eastern shallows and has been growing ever since.

The Sea-Mire is not malicious. It is not even fully sentient. It is an animal, a juvenile of a species that has only one adult member, and it behaves the way any large predator in a small territory behaves. It eats what it can catch, it surfaces to breathe even though it does not need to, and it attacks anything that enters the water. It has been growing for four hundred years. If it continues to grow, it will eventually outsize the eastern shallows entirely, and then it will spill into the central lake, and then it will be the king's problem. But that is a future that has not yet been woven.

## Part VII: The Faiths of the Long Shore

### 24. What the Dead Believe

Religion on Jörmund is not what it was in the living world. The dead arrive with their faiths intact, devotees of Odin, worshippers of Freyja, thralls who prayed to Thor for strength, priests who burned offerings to the Allfather, and within a century, those faiths corrode. The gods cannot enter Jörmund. The gods cannot hear prayers from inside the threshold. The dead pray, and no one answers.

This has produced four distinct responses among the dead.

The Faithful are a minority. They continue to worship as they did in life, maintaining shrines, speaking prayers, carving runes of devotion into the island's stone. They acknowledge that the gods cannot reach them, but they believe the gods are still watching, that the arches are not just doorways but windows, and that the gods see everything that happens on the long shore even if they cannot act. The Faithful are concentrated in the southern settlements, where the arches are closest and the sense of connection to the outside is strongest. They are not delusional. They are stubborn, in the way that faith is always stubborn, and their stubbornness is one of the few things on the island that the ferals cannot touch.

The Bitter are a larger group. They arrived as devout worshippers and have been broken by the silence. They do not pray. They do not maintain shrines. Some have turned their anger on the Faithful, mocking their devotions. Some have turned it inward. The Bitter are concentrated in the northern settlements, where the distance from the arches is greatest and the sense of isolation is sharpest.

The Converted have transferred their devotion from the gods to the Norns. They do not worship the Norns, worship implies supplication, and the Norns do not accept supplication. But they revere them as the true architects of existence, the only powers that actually operate on the long shore. The fortune-teller tradition is a Converted practice. Rune-reading as inherited from the Norns is treated as a sacred craft, a way of maintaining connection to the only beings who have ever demonstrably influenced the fate of the dead.

The Hollow are the largest group and the quietest. They no longer believe in anything. Not the gods, not the Norns, not the king, not the Pool. They exist. They eat the dim food and sleep the small sleep and walk the shore and wait for nothing. They are not feral. They still have their names, their languages, their memories. They are simply empty. The Hollow are the majority of the background NPCs the player encounters, the shuffling dead who populate the towns, who speak in tired sentences, who have nothing to offer because they have nothing left to believe in. They are the human cost of Hjarrandi's refusal, measured not in violence but in absence.

### 25. Religious Practices

Rune-carving is the most widespread practice on the island, used by the Faithful, the Converted, and even some of the Bitter out of habit. Runes are carved into stone, wood, bone, and occasionally skin. The carvings serve two purposes: communication, the dead write messages to each other using the runes as an alphabet, and invocation, the dead use the runes as the Norns originally intended them, as shapes that resonate with the structure of fate. The player's runestone system is a weaponized version of the same practice.

Arch-touching. Newly arrived souls instinctively reach out to touch the stone of the arch they passed through. This is not a religious act, exactly. It is more like a reflex, the way a drowning man grabs for something solid. But over the centuries, the act has been formalized. The dead touch the arches when they are afraid. They touch them when they are lost. They touch them when they want to feel connected to the world they left. The arches are warm, not physically, but in a way the dead can sense, a residual warmth from the passage of millions of souls.

Name-speaking. The dead speak their names aloud every morning, a practice recommended in the Act I voices. This is not a religious act but a survival practice. The feral degradation that turns a soul into a kobold or a wolf begins with the loss of the name. The name is the anchor of identity, and speaking it aloud reaffirms the anchor. The practice is universal. Even the Bitter, who have abandoned every other ritual, speak their names.

Pool-gazing. Before the damming, the dead would gather at the shore of the central lake and watch the Pool stir. The stirring was the Pool's way of signaling activity, a soul being unmade, a life being woven, the cycle turning. Watching it was a meditative practice, a way of contemplating the transition that awaited them. Since the damming, the Pool is still, and Pool-gazing has become a form of mourning. The dead sit at the lakeshore and stare at the unmoving water and remember what it used to do.

Ash-scattering. When a dead soul is destroyed, the body dissolves into black ash and reforms at the nearest activated arch overnight. The ash left behind is considered sacred by the Faithful, not because it is divine, but because it is the physical residue of a soul's suffering, and the Faithful believe that suffering should be acknowledged. Ash-scattering is the practice of gathering the ash and carrying it to a high place, a ridge, a cliff, the roof of a building, and releasing it into the air. It does not scatter far. There is no wind on Jörmund. But the gesture is the point.

## Part VIII: The Feral Gradient

### 26. How Souls Go Wrong, In Detail

The degradation from person to feral is not instant. It is a gradient, a slow descent through recognizable stages, and the stages are visible in the enemy hierarchy the player fights through.

Stage one: the waiting. A soul arrives on Jörmund whole, with its name, its memories, its language, its sense of self. It eats, sleeps, speaks, feels. It is, in every way that matters, a person. Most souls remain at this stage for their entire time on the shore, however long that may be. They are the shuffling dead, the background NPCs, the residents of the towns. They are tired, and sad, and sometimes angry, but they are themselves.

Stage two: the fraying. After a prolonged period of waiting, the duration varies wildly depending on the strength of the soul's identity, the edges begin to blur. Memories go first. The faces of children, the names of parents, the layout of the village they lived in. Then language. Words drop out of sentences, replaced by grunts or silences. Then social behavior. The fraying soul withdraws from the communities, preferring solitude, because the presence of other people reminds them of what they are losing. The fraying soul still knows its name. It speaks the name every morning, the way the practice demands. But the name is starting to feel like a sound rather than an identity, a noise the mouth makes rather than a truth the self holds.

Stage three: the dropping. The feral-trigger. This is the moment the name is lost, spoken for the last time, forgotten, replaced by a blank. Without the name, the identity collapses. The soul's form begins to change. The posture slumps. The jaw loosens. The eyes go white. It drops to all fours, not because its body has changed shape, but because the human posture, upright, forward-facing, head raised, is a learned behavior that requires the self to maintain, and the self is gone. The dropped soul flees the community and runs into the wilderness, driven by instinct. Hunger, fear, the desire to find warmth. It is no longer a person. It is a feral.

Stage four: the shaping. The island takes over. Jörmund shapes the forgotten into what it needs, or, more accurately, into what the soul's last memories suggest. A soul that died in winter and remembers only cold becomes a wolf. A soul that died young and remembers only smallness becomes a kobold. A soul that died angry and remembers only the desire to hit something becomes a goblin, and then a hobgoblin, and then an orc. The progression is not always linear, a kobold does not necessarily become a goblin, but the general direction is clear: the longer a feral survives, the more the island shapes it, and the more it is shaped, the less of the original person remains.

The final stage, the orc, the brown bear, the creatures that are barely distinguishable from the island's native animals, represents total dissolution of the human self. If such a soul were to walk into the Pool, the Pool would accept it, but it cannot follow the pull, because following requires a self, and so it remains, hunting and howling and existing, until the cycle resumes and the Pool's flow can reach even the most dissolved of the forgotten.

## Part IX: The Key NPCs

### 27. The Residents of the Towns

Smith Hela arrived through the West Arch approximately 900 years before the present. In life, she was a blacksmith in a coastal village, not a warrior-smith forging swords, but a practical smith making nails, hinges, pot-hooks, and plowshares. She took the name Hela as a child, given it by a father who worshipped the death-goddess and wanted his daughter to carry her strength. On Jörmund, the name became a burden. Every soul she met assumed she was the goddess herself, and she spent her first century on the shore correcting them.

She belongs to the second category of soul-corruption, those who take a god's name as a shield. But unlike Skadi or Heimdall's Echo, Hela took the name before she died, in humility rather than desperation, and the difference has protected her. She has not twisted. She is angry, sharp-tongued, and profoundly tired, but she is still herself. She forges weapons that bite the dead because she has spent nine centuries thinking about what the dead are made of, and she understands the substance of black blood better than anyone on the island except the Norns.

She has opinions about the king. She knows what hoarding does to a soul, because she has watched it happen to the name she carries. Every time someone calls her Hela and flinches, she sees a mirror of Hjarrandi's choice. She is on the player's side because the player is the only person on the island who has never called her by her father's goddess.

Bath-keeper Aud arrived through the South Arch approximately 1,200 years before the present. In life, she was a healer, a village woman who knew which herbs drew out infection and which mushrooms eased a fever. She drowned crossing a river in flood, carrying a sick child on her back. The child survived. She did not. On Jörmund, she found that her healing skills were useless. The dead cannot be healed, because they are not sick. They are simply dead, and their black blood is not a wound but a condition. The one thing she could do was wash them. She built a bath-house of collected stone and heated the water with coals from the volcanic vents to the west. It is the gentlest place on the island.

Aud is the first NPC to notice the player is alive, because she is the first person on Jörmund whose craft requires touching someone closely enough to feel their warmth. A dead soul is cool to the touch. The player is warm. Aud notices this the way a healer notices a fever: instantly, instinctively, with a mix of wonder and concern.

Merchant Vel arrived through the East Arch approximately 400 years before the present, almost exactly when Hjarrandi did, though they came through different doors. In life, he was a trader who sailed the northern routes between Iceland and the Faroe Islands. He died when his knarr went down in a storm. On Jörmund, he continued to trade, because trading is the only thing he knows how to do. He has no moral position on the king, the Pool, or the player. He buys and sells. He has survived four centuries of stagnation by refusing to care about anything that cannot be exchanged for something else. This is not a virtue. It is a coping mechanism that has calcified into a personality.

Fortune-teller Ylla has lived in Storsprunga for approximately 600 years. In life, she was a farm-wife with no gift for rune-craft. On Jörmund, she was taught the basics of rune-reading by a wandering soul who had learned from a soul who had learned from a soul who had, five or six generations of transmission back, learned from Verðandi herself. Ylla is a competent practitioner. She reads the runes honestly and well. She does not claim any special authority, and she is slightly dismissive of the lineage when asked about it. "Some old woman taught an old woman who taught an old woman. The shapes hold. That is all that matters."

Fortune-teller Svala has lived in Nasus for approximately 700 years. In life, she was a völva, a seeress, a practitioner of seiðr, a woman who saw the threads of fate and spoke of them in verse. On Jörmund, she sought out the rune-craft tradition with purpose, tracing the chain of transmission back as far as she could. She wears her lineage like armor. She carves her runes with ceremony, speaks her readings in verse, and carries herself with the gravity of a woman who believes she is doing sacred work. She is not wrong. But her pride is also a shield against the terror of her situation, a woman who defined herself by seeing the future, trapped on an island where the future has stopped.

Blacksmith Keld arrived through the North Arch approximately 500 years before the present. In life, he was a Sami reindeer herder who learned metalwork from a traveling Norwegian smith. He is not a warrior-smith like Hela. He is a mender, a patcher, a man who fixes what is broken because leaving broken things broken offends him. His forge in Nasus produces functional tools and basic weapons, nothing elegant, nothing enchanted, but reliable.

### 28. The Observer

The Observer is the oldest named soul who still speaks. He has been at the South Arch for four hundred years, perhaps longer. He is the first NPC the player meets, and the voice the game calibrates all other voices against.

He knew Hjarrandi. Not in life, but on the shore. He was at the South Arch when the warlord walked through, and he watched him cross the island, and he watched him build, and he watched him refuse. He does not say this at first. He withholds Hjarrandi's name, he withholds his own history, he withholds everything except the bare facts the player needs to survive the next stretch of road. He does this not out of cruelty but out of care. Names are weight. The dead who hold them grow strange. The dead who lose them grow worse. He will not give the player a weight they are not ready to carry.

What the player does not know, and what the Observer may never say, is that the Observer did not choose to stand at the arch. He was drawn there. Something in the arch's warmth, in the residual passage of millions of souls, holds him in place the way a campfire holds a freezing man. He has tried to leave. He has walked away, once, twice, a dozen times. Each time he has come back, not because the arch pulls him, but because the shore beyond the arch is empty and cold and the arch is the only place on the island that still feels like a doorway, and a doorway is the closest thing to hope that the dead possess.

## Part XI: Critic's Notes

These are places where I made choices that you may want to override.

The Observer. I deliberately left his identity open in this draft rather than committing to the father-of-Hjarrandi reveal. It is still there as an option, it still works, but it is not baked in. The text describes him as someone who knew Hjarrandi on the shore, not in life, which is compatible with the father reading or with a dozen other readings. If you want the father reveal, the only change needed is a single late-game dialogue line. If you do not, nothing in this document contradicts that choice.

Hrímfaxi's Get. I kept the full backstory, the mother whose children chose the Pool without her. The claw-marks-as-names detail closes the mystery. If you prefer to leave the mystery open, the backstory can be stripped to just the mechanical details and the line "she is protecting something, and what she is protecting is a question the dead have argued about for a very long time."

The Feral Gradient. The four-stage process is my framework for the enemy hierarchy. If you want the forms to be more chaotic and less systematic, collapse the stages and let the island's shaping be random rather than memory-driven.

The new settlements. Brann's Crucible, the Ashfield, the Driftworks, and Hollowmere are additions. They fill gaps in the geography and give the player more environmental storytelling to discover. If any of them feel wrong for the map, they can be removed without affecting the rest of the lore. Hollowmere in particular exists to serve the purpose you identified: a settlement close to the giant and the mountains, which makes more geographic sense as the community that directly experienced Gymir's stirring.

The Pool. I have described it as something no one fully understands. What it is made of, no one can say. It looks like water. It might be something older. The mystery is intact.

The arches. They are now part of the Norns' original design, not the gods' work. The gods have had no hand in Jörmund.

The tower. It is immaculate. Four hundred years of tireless labor by builders with nothing else to do. The most beautiful thing on the island, built by people who chose building over the water.
